### Social Traders definition framework

Social Traders describes a social enterprise as a business that exists to create public or community benefit. Its five internationally recognised features are: purpose (solving a social or environmental problem); operations (prioritising purpose, people, and planet in daily decisions); revenue (a self-sustaining model); use of surplus (the majority reinvested in the mission); and structure (legal and financial arrangements that lock in purpose over time).

Use this as a selection and adoption check for a problem or prototype, rather than as a claim that a hackathon project must itself be a certified social enterprise or that the five features are the event's published scoring rubric.

### Twelve recent Australian problem opportunities

These are problem areas, not permission to automate high-stakes decisions. For family violence, housing eligibility, health, crisis or benefits, AI should remain a bounded assistive layer with informed consent, clear escalation and human authority.

1. **Emergency accommodation availability and referral failure.** AIHW recorded about 129,000 unassisted Specialist Homelessness Services requests in 2024–25; 60% were for short-term or emergency accommodation.
2. **Long-term housing pathway gaps.** Of clients needing long-term housing assistance, 72% were neither provided nor referred that service in 2024–25.
3. **Safe housing after family and domestic violence.** Around 117,000 Specialist Homelessness Services clients had experienced family and domestic violence in 2024–25; safety and privacy must be non-negotiable.
4. **Homelessness and mental-health service hand-offs.** Mental-health support needs are common in homelessness services, yet specialist support can be unmet; focus on verified referral and follow-up, not diagnosis.
5. **Youth cost-of-living and housing pressure.** In Mission Australia’s 2025 Youth Survey, cost of living was the top national concern (64%), with mental health, climate and housing also prominent.
6. **Food insecurity and dignity-preserving access.** Foodbank estimated about 3.5 million Australians experienced food insecurity in the previous 12 months in its 2025 report.
7. **Internet affordability and access.** The 2025 Australian Digital Inclusion Index found around one in five Australians digitally excluded or highly excluded; affordability stress is material.
8. **Accessible digital service use for older people and people with disability.** The same index identifies high exclusion among people aged 75+ and disproportionate mobile-only access among people with disability.
9. **First Nations digital inclusion in remote communities.** The 2025 Mapping the Digital Gap reporting shows especially high exclusion in remote and very remote communities; co-design and community data authority are essential.
10. **Regional and remote service reach.** Homelessness-service use and supply vary with remoteness, local service availability, housing supply and affordability; low-bandwidth, multi-channel delivery matters.
11. **Climate-disaster information, preparedness and recovery.** Australia’s National Climate Risk Assessment identifies linked risks for communities, health and social support, infrastructure, food and water.
12. **Impact measurement and social-procurement readiness.** SECNA is testing tools so social enterprises can measure and communicate outcomes consistently, efficiently and effectively; a product must make claims auditable rather than generate persuasive but unsupported impact language.

## Australian research led interview domains

Do not treat these as thirteen features to put in one product. They are separate problem spaces for discovery. Choose one narrow service moment after interviewing the relevant workers or organisations.

### 1. Emergency accommodation availability and referral hand-offs

Interview Specialist Homelessness Services workers, intake staff and accommodation providers. Reconstruct the last request that failed: information freshness, eligibility, actual capacity, referral status, repeated storytelling and who owns the next step.

Safety: start with workers and organisations. Do not recruit people currently experiencing homelessness without a partner organisation and appropriate ethics and safeguards.

### 2. Long-term housing and tenancy support pathways

Interview housing advocates, tenancy sustainment workers and social-housing support teams. Validate document burden, queue uncertainty, missed follow-ups, referral loops and what a worker can truthfully promise.

Do not automate allocation, priority scoring or eligibility. A credible scope is case preparation, transparent next-step tracking or human-led referral coordination.

### 3. Safety-first family and domestic violence housing support

Interview only trained family and domestic violence workers or organisations that agree to participate. Validate safe contact preferences, information disclosure risks, service hand-off failure and the limits of any digital record.

This is not a suitable direct-to-survivor student interview topic without a specialist partner. Never expose locations, infer safety risk, or create a tool that can be accessed by a perpetrator.

### 4. Housing and mental-health service continuity

Interview homelessness case workers, community mental-health organisations and peer-support coordinators. Investigate missed appointments, duplicated intake, referrals that do not complete, and which information can safely travel between services.

AI may support a verified checklist, consent-aware summary or follow-up reminder. It must not diagnose, triage a crisis or decide clinical urgency.

### 5. Youth cost-of-living, housing and service navigation

Interview youth-service workers, financial counsellors, student support teams and community centres. Ask about the latest case where a young person had several linked needs: rent, food, transport, mental-health support or paperwork.

Validate the moment a person drops out of the process and whether the barrier is awareness, documents, timing, trust, transport or eligibility.

### 6. Food insecurity and dignity-preserving access

Interview food-relief coordinators, pantry volunteers and community-service staff. Map how people discover support, book or collect it, deal with transport and preserve privacy.

Foodbank evidence makes stigma and embarrassment an explicit research question. Do not assume that a map of food locations solves access.

### 7. Internet affordability, devices and low-bandwidth access

Interview digital-inclusion organisations, public-library staff and frontline service workers. Establish whether people have a stable device, enough data, a safe email or phone number, and the ability to complete a multi-step online form.

Design questions should cover offline fallback, SMS or phone alternatives and data-light flows, not just translation quality.

### 8. Accessible digital service use for older people and people with disability

Interview disability advocates, accessible-service providers and organisations supporting older people. Observe which steps fail with screen readers, voice input, small touch targets, cognitive load or complex service language.

Test whether voice-first, simplified language, human support and fewer steps change task completion. Do not generalise accessibility needs from one user.

### 9. First Nations digital inclusion and service design

Only pursue this domain with an Aboriginal Community Controlled Organisation or another appropriate First Nations partner. Ask the partner what problem is worth solving, what data should stay local and what governance is needed.

Do not start from a preselected AI solution, treat language translation as sufficient, or collect community data without a partner-led purpose and authority.

### 10. Regional and remote service reach

Interview regional or remote service providers, not only city-based organisations. Validate travel distance, low connectivity, staff coverage, local service capacity and disruption during weather events.

The useful prototype may be a low-bandwidth workflow, local service-coverage view or human escalation path rather than a centralised chatbot.

### 11. Climate-disaster service continuity and recovery

Interview community-resilience organisations, disaster-recovery services or local government teams. Map what fails before, during and after an event: warnings, contact information, supply access, service capacity and recovery referrals.

Keep claims narrow. The product should turn verified local information into action, not claim to predict disasters or make emergency decisions.

### 12. Administrative and case-processing burden in small social enterprises

Interview founders, operations staff, volunteers and program coordinators. Investigate repeated email, form, intake, scheduling, volunteer matching, data entry and internal knowledge work that reduces direct-service time.

This remains one of the lowest-risk opportunities for a two-day workflow prototype: constrain AI to extraction, drafting, classification, retrieval or a human-approved follow-up.

### 13. Impact measurement, verification and social-procurement readiness

Interview program managers, grant writers, social-enterprise founders and procurement-facing staff. Investigate which outputs are collected, which outcomes lack evidence, how long reporting takes, and what verification buyers or funders require.

Measure a defensible workflow result such as evidence-collection time, missing-data rate or report-review time. Never let AI invent outcomes, attribution or impact claims.

## Interview rules

- Ask about the most recent real example, not hypothetical opinions.
- Reconstruct the current workflow step by step.
- Ask what happens when the process fails and who pays the cost.
- Measure frequency, time, error, delay, completion, and workarounds.
- Do not pitch the AI solution during the problem interview.
- Obtain permission before recording or quoting.
- De-identify sensitive notes.
- External outreach or messaging requires Tommy’s explicit approval before sending.

## Recommended strategic direction

Prioritise one high-friction workflow inside a specific social enterprise and connect it to measurable impact evidence.

Strong candidate structure:

> A specific social-enterprise worker loses time or creates risk at one step of an existing service workflow. The product uses AI for a constrained task, shows its source/confidence, requires human approval for high-risk action, and records an outcome that the organisation can use for learning and reporting.

If the team chooses homelessness, prefer a complementary layer such as service-data freshness, eligibility checking, human referral, or outcome tracking. Do not directly clone Moreneta’s public AI-assistant concept.

## Build and pitch order

1. Write the problem as one user, one moment, one blocked outcome.
2. Validate the current process through interviews.
3. Identify the highest-cost or highest-risk break point.
4. Design the ideal human workflow first.
5. Assign AI only the constrained classification, retrieval, extraction, summarisation, recommendation, or warning task it can justify.
6. Build one complete golden path.
7. Add one low-confidence or failure path.
8. Test with a small labelled set or human evaluation rubric.
9. Pitch the user and working demo before the architecture.
10. Explain adoption, maintenance, cost, privacy, safety, and outcome measurement.

- Five half-built features instead of one complete workflow.
